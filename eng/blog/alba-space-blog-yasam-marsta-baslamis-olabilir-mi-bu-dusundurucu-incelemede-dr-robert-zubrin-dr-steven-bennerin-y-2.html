In this thought-provoking review, Dr. Robert Zubrin examines Dr. Steven Benner's new book and how he tackles one of the biggest questions in planetary science. By Dr. Robert Zubrin, National Review, 07.11.26 Steve Benner is one of the leading figures in both theoretical and applied biology. As one of the founders of synthetic biology, Benner was the first person to synthesize a gene and is also a pioneer of the RNA world theory of the origin of life. He is also a successful biotech entrepreneur; this is important for him because his wealth gives him the freedom to speak his mind, even when his views clash with those of NASA and other members of the scientific community. His magnificent new book "Meet the Neighbors: Life on Mars and How to Find It" is a case in point: Benner, drawing on his own research and encyclopedic knowledge of the work of the astrobiology community, makes bold conclusions without compromise. Gathering impressive evidence, Benner argues that life almost certainly exists on Mars and that life emerged on the Red Planet before it did on Earth. NASA's public relations department has always claimed that their goal is to search for life in their various Mars missions. They said this because they knew that the American public is most interested in the possibility of extraterrestrial life when it comes to Mars. Unfortunately, NASA has failed to deliver on this promise. In fact, the US space agency has not sent any instrument to Mars to detect life since the Viking mission, which landed on the Red Planet on July 20, 1976, nearly half a century ago. Since then, NASA has sent orbiters, landers, and rovers to Mars. While these missions have obtained very useful data about the Martian environment, none of them have attempted to search for life itself. However, the Viking landers also carried a fourth instrument, known as the Gas Chromatograph Mass Spectrometer (GCMS). The GCMS evaluated the chemical composition of the soil and did not detect any organic matter in the Martian soil. This was quite surprising for NASA scientists, as they expected to find some organic matter on Mars, even if there was no life - at least from meteorites, which often contain robust organic compounds called polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).
