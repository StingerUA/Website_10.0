The Panoramic Eye of the Universe: Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

Aslı Bolat · Astronomer and Educator · September 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Roman Space Telescope

What we see when we look at the sky is actually only a small part of the universe. With billions of galaxies, stars, planets, and cosmic events happening simultaneously, astronomers are trying to make sense of this huge landscape.

Now we have a new eye that can see the sky from a much wider angle.

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope was launched into space on August 30, 2026, aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket. After a journey of about three months, it is planned to reach the Sun-Earth system's orbit around L2, the second Lagrangian point, where it will begin scanning the universe. NASA expects the first images of Roman to be released in early 2027.

So what makes Roman so special?

The answer is actually quite simple:

Rather than taking a closer look at the universe, the novel will try to see a much larger part of the universe at once.

The Hubble Space Telescope has given us some of the most iconic images of the universe. The James Webb Space Telescope, on the other hand, provides new insights into the early universe and the formation of stars, galaxies and planetary systems, especially with its infrared observations.

The novel comes to solve a different problem? How wide of the sky can we see all these details at the same time?

Roman's 2.4-meter main mirror has the same diameter as Hubble's mirror. However, the wide-field camera, called the Wide Field Instrument (WFI), will be able to observe a region at least 100 times wider than Hubble's field of view in a single image. Moreover, it will be able to scan much larger areas while maintaining a resolution close to Hubble's image quality.

This difference doesn't just mean bigger photos in terms of astronomy. Roman's real strength will be revealed in statistics.

Studying a single galaxy in detail can tell us a lot about that galaxy. But studying millions or even billions of galaxies in the same way can reveal a much bigger picture of how the universe behaves.

According to NASA, Roman is expected to measure light from about a billion galaxies during its mission lifetime. So while Hubble and Webb will give us cosmic portraits, Roman will make a cosmic map on a large scale.

Chasing the Mysterious Part of the 95% of the Universe

One of Roman's biggest scientific goals is to investigate perhaps two of the biggest questions in modern cosmology: What is dark matter and how does dark energy behave?

Most of the content of the universe consists of components that we cannot see directly. Dark energy makes up about 68% of the energy content of the universe according to the current cosmological model and is associated with the acceleration of the expansion of the universe. However, its nature is still unknown.

This is where the novel will come into play. The telescope will investigate how matter is distributed in the universe by studying the distribution of billions of galaxies and the very small changes in their shapes. Thanks to the effect known as gravitational lensing, information can be obtained about how dark matter, which we cannot see directly, is distributed on large scales.

At the same time, cosmological measurements such as supernovae and galaxy distributions will be used to investigate more precisely how the universe expands over time.

At the end of all this, the question that astronomers will try to answer is quite big: Why is the universe expanding faster and faster? And more importantly:

Does dark energy really behave the way we think it does?

If Roman's observations reveal results that do not agree with current models of cosmology, this may not only be a new discovery, but a development that may cause us to rethink the way we explain the universe.

Roman's story is not limited to cosmology. One of the important targets of the telescope is exoplanets.

Roman will perform a statistical census of planetary systems by observing particularly dense star fields of the Milky Way. One of the most important methods of this is gravitational microlensing.

In this method, a foreground star gravitationally bends and magnifies the light of another star behind it. If there is a planet around the foreground star, this planet can also cause a short-term change in the light curve.

By capturing this small shift, astronomers can detect the presence of a planet that is too distant for them to image directly.

Roman's microlensing research is expected to discover thousands of exoplanets. Moreover, this method will be an important tool for searching for planets in more distant orbits and some stray planets that are not connected to star systems, not limited to planets very close to their stars.

Another technology Roman carries on him is the Coronagraph Instrument. One of the biggest problems with directly imaging a planet is that the star illuminating it, not the planet's own light, is extremely bright.

The purpose of the coronagraph is precisely to test technologies to solve this problem: to suppress the bright light coming from the star and distinguish the light coming from the much fainter planets around the star.

Roman's coronagraph will test advanced technologies developed for direct imaging and spectroscopy of exoplanets in space. Therefore, Roman will not only make discoveries; it will also test technologies that could pave the way for more advanced space telescopes in the future.

Roman's Journey Has Just Begun

The story of the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope did not actually end with the launch on August 30; on the contrary, it began.

Roman is currently heading towards the L2 region, about 1.5 million kilometers away. This location will allow the telescope to manage the light and heat conditions coming from the Sun and Earth in a more controlled manner and operate with low fuel consumption.

Roman's basic mission is planned for five years, and an extended mission period beyond this is possible under favorable conditions. The telescope's fuel will be one of the important factors limiting its mission life. Moreover, the amount of data Roman will send is impressive in itself.

NASA expects the telescope to send about 1.4 terabytes of data per day. Machine learning, artificial intelligence and citizen science projects are also planned to be used to process this data.

Looking Wider at the Sky

For centuries, humanity looked at the sky with naked eyes. Then we improved telescopes. Hubble showed us details of the universe that we had never seen before. James Webb looked deeper into the cosmic past with his infrared eyes. Now Roman is preparing to do something else:

It will allow us to see a much larger part of the universe at once. Maybe Roman's greatest discovery will be a new galaxy, a new planet, or a new cosmic event. Maybe he'll find an unexpected clue to the nature of dark energy. Maybe he'll discover thousands of new planets.

Or it will reveal a completely different phenomenon that we do not even know exists today.

The journey of the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is exactly the story of this.
