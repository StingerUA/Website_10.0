How Is Data About a Planet Collected?

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Author · August 26, 2026 · 5 min read

Everything we know about a planet—what its atmosphere contains, what its surface looks like, whether there is water beneath it—ultimately comes from sequences of numbers. But the way those numbers are collected splits into two very different approaches depending on where the planet is.

On one side are the planets in the Solar System: we can send spacecraft to them. On the other are exoplanets, planets orbiting other stars: we cannot travel to them, so we can only observe them from afar.

This distinction may sound simple, but it fundamentally changes the way data is collected.

Exoplanets: Observing Indirectly

Most exoplanets have never actually been seen directly. We infer a planet's existence from the tiny drop in a star's brightness as the planet passes in front of it, or from the slight wobble caused as the planet gravitationally pulls on its star.

In other words, we do not measure the planet itself; we measure the planet's effect on its star.

It is a little like trying to figure out who is inside a room without entering it, simply by looking at the shadow beneath the door.

One of the most important methods used to study exoplanets is the transit method. When a planet passes in front of its star, the amount of starlight reaching us decreases by a very small amount. If this decrease repeats at regular intervals, it can provide strong evidence for the existence of a planet.

Another method measures the planet's gravitational effect on its star. The planet pulls the star slightly toward itself, producing a small motion. This movement can be detected through changes in the star's light.

So collecting data about exoplanets relies less on looking directly at the planet and more on reading the traces it leaves on its star.

The Solar System: Measuring on Site

With our planetary neighbors, we have a much greater luxury: we can take the instrument directly there.

These spacecraft operate in several different ways.

Orbiters

Orbiters travel around a planet and scan its surface for years. The work performed by the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter for more than fifteen years is one example.

These spacecraft can study a planet's surface using different cameras and sensors, map large areas, and monitor changes over time.

Landers and Rovers

Landers and rovers touch down directly on the surface, make contact with the soil, drill into rocks, and collect samples when necessary.

This method allows much more direct measurements than observing a planet's surface from afar. It can provide detailed information ranging from the chemical composition of a rock to the physical properties of the soil.

Flyby Missions

Flyby missions pass rapidly by a planet or another celestial body only once.

The Voyager spacecraft's encounters with the outer planets are among the best-known examples. In a flyby, there is only one chance: scientists get only as much information as the spacecraft can collect during that brief encounter.

What Instruments Are Used to Study Planets?

The instruments carried by spacecraft are not all the same.

Cameras take images in visible light to create pictures of the surface and surrounding environment.

Spectrometers separate light coming from the surface or atmosphere into different wavelengths, providing information about which elements, minerals, or molecules may be present.

Radar instruments are used to look beneath the surface. They can provide especially valuable information when investigating ice, rock, or subsurface structures.

Magnetometers measure a planet's magnetic field. These measurements can help us learn about the planet's internal structure and history.

Seismometers listen to vibrations within a planet. By studying how these vibrations propagate, scientists can obtain information about the planet's interior.

So the data collected by a spacecraft consists of far more than photographs. In reality, each instrument is designed to answer a different question about the planet.

Before Going to the Planet: Rehearsals on Earth

So how is all of this tested on Earth?

You cannot send an instrument millions of kilometers away and simply say, “I hope it works.”

That is why every instrument is tested for months in environments designed to reproduce the conditions at its destination as closely as possible.

Analog Sites

One part of this testing takes place at analog sites.

The Atacama Desert in Chile is one of the important regions used to simulate the dry and harsh environment of Mars.

Iceland's volcanic plains are used to study basaltic surfaces and simulate different planetary environments.

Northern Canadian islands provide important locations for studies that reproduce frozen ground and cold-climate conditions.

Some of the first “walks” of rovers—that is, parts of their mobility tests and mission scenarios—can be tested in these kinds of analog environments.

Laboratory Tests

The other major part takes place in the laboratory.

In thermal vacuum chambers, instruments are exposed to the low-pressure conditions of space as well as extremely high and low temperatures.

On vibration tables, engineers simulate the vibrations and mechanical stresses that may occur during launch.

The goal is not merely to confirm that the instrument works, but to make sure it can keep working during launch, throughout the journey through space, and after reaching its target planet.

Calibration Targets

Each instrument also travels with a calibration target.

This may be a small color or mineral reference plate whose properties have been measured with great precision on Earth.

When the spacecraft reaches the planet, it first observes this target. This lets us check whether the values it measures are truly accurate and whether anything during the long journey has changed the instrument in a way that could affect its measurement accuracy.

The goal is simple:

Never send something millions of kilometers away with nothing more than “I hope it works.”

What Happens After the Data Arrives?

What reaches Earth from the spacecraft is not yet a photograph or a scientific conclusion.

Raw data must pass through a fairly long processing pipeline.

We can think of this process in six basic steps.

1. Transmission

The data is sent to Earth by radio waves and captured by giant antennas.

The distance is enormous and bandwidth is limited. For this reason, data is usually recorded, compressed, and transmitted back to Earth in pieces during suitable communication windows.

Even a signal from Mars takes about 3 to 22 minutes to reach Earth. The exact time depends on the positions of the two planets in their orbits.

2. Cleaning

Raw measurements can contain various imperfections caused by the detectors and instruments themselves.

For example:

  • Dead pixels
  • Heat-related noise
  • Detector errors
  • Differences in how lenses collect light near the edges of an image
  • Data packets corrupted during transmission

Problems like these are identified.

Known instrument defects are removed from the data or corrected. If any packets were corrupted during transmission, they are also detected and flagged.

3. Calibration

Next, the data is calibrated.

This is where the calibration target mentioned earlier becomes important.

Raw numbers do not mean very much by themselves.

For example:

“This pixel returned a value of 3184.”

That number is not directly a scientific result.

After calibration, the value can be converted into a meaningful physical quantity:

a particular brightness, temperature, or reflectance measured at a specific wavelength.

In other words, a raw number generated electronically by the instrument is transformed into a measurement that describes the physical world.

4. Positioning

Data by itself is not enough.

We must also know where it belongs and when it was measured.

The spacecraft's orbital and positional information is combined with the measurements. In this way, every data point can be linked to a specific coordinate on the planet's surface and to a timestamp.

Only after this stage can images be placed correctly on a map.

Instead of merely saying, “there was this color here,” scientists can say:

“At this coordinate on the planet, on this date, this property was measured.”

5. Archiving

The resulting data is then uploaded to publicly accessible scientific archives.

Examples include NASA's Planetary Data System (PDS) and ESA's Planetary Science Archive (PSA).

This step is much more important than it may appear.

Even years or decades after a space mission ends, scientists can return to old data and study it again.

New scientific studies and papers are still being produced today from data collected by Voyager in the 1980s.

6. Interpretation

In the final stage, scientists compare the archived data with physical and mathematical models.

The findings are analyzed, scientific papers are prepared, and the results are subjected to peer review.

For a result to be considered a genuinely reliable discovery, it usually needs to be confirmed through independent observations or different methods.

In this way, a raw measurement becomes knowledge through a controlled scientific process.

From Raw Data to Knowledge

In summary, the path from spacecraft data to scientific knowledge follows this sequence:

Transmission → Cleaning → Calibration → Positioning → Archiving → Interpretation

At first glance, many of these steps may seem highly technical and even boring.

But much of what we know about planets exists precisely because of this process.

Closing

Perhaps my favorite thing about this entire chain is this:

None of the individual steps sounds especially exciting on its own.

Fixing a bad pixel, converting a unit, labeling a file, or assigning a measurement to the correct coordinate does not sound like a major discovery.

But the reason we can say that Mars once had lakes or that there is an ocean beneath Europa's ice is precisely because these seemingly boring steps were carried out patiently and carefully.

And that data is still there.

The archives are open. The data can be accessed and examined again.

The person who makes the next discovery does not necessarily need to send a new spacecraft.

Perhaps all they need is to open a file that no one has looked at closely before.
