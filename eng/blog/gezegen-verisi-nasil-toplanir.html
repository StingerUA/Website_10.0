How to Collect Planetary Data

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · August 26, 2026 · 7 min read

Everything we know about a planet ultimately comes out of a string of numbers. An article about where that series of numbers came from and what steps it went through until it turned into information. Everything we know about a planet — what its atmosphere is, what its surface looks like, whether or not it has water underneath — ultimately comes from a string of numbers. But how that string of numbers is added up is divided into two from the beginning according to where the planet is. On the one hand, there are the planets in the Solar System: we can send vehicles there. On the other side, exoplanets, i.e. those around other stars: we can never go to them, we can only look at them from afar. This distinction seems simple, but it radically changes the work of data collection. Exoplanets: looking indirectly We have never actually seen most of the exoplanets. We can tell the existence of a planet by the very small drop in brightness it creates as it passes in front of its star, or by the tremor it creates when it pulls the star slightly towards itself. So we're not measuring the planet, but the planet's effect on its star — it's like walking into a room, looking at the shadow under the door to figure out who's inside. Solar System: measure in situ In our own neighbors, we have a much more luxurious possibility: we can take the instrument directly there. These tools work in several ways. Orbiters orbit the planet and scan the surface for years; That's what Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has been doing for over fifteen years. Landers and rovers land directly on the surface and touch the soil, drill rocks, take samples. Flyby missions speed past it only once — like the Voyagers' visit to exoplanets; You have only one chance, whatever you collect in those few hours. The tools on them are not uniform either. Cameras take pictures in visible light. Spectrometers separate the light reflected from the surface into its colors and tell which minerals are present. Radars look below the surface. Magnetometers measure the planet's magnetic field — which gives an idea of whether its core is still liquid. Seismometers, on the other hand, listen to vibrations within the planet itself. So how are they tested on Earth? It is not possible to send a tool millions of kilometers away and say "I hope it works". That's why each device is tested for months in environments where its destination is imitated. Each device goes through three separate tests on Earth before going into space. One leg of this is analogue fields: the Atacama Desert in Chile mimics the dryness of Mars, the volcanic plains of Iceland mimic basalt surfaces, and the northern islands of Canada mimic permafrost. Travelers take their first walks here. The other leg is the laboratory: in thermal vacuum chambers, the device is exposed to the pressure and temperature extremes of space, and in shaking tables, the vibration of the launch is simulated. And with each instrument is a calibration target — a small slab of color or minerals, the properties of which are measured in millimeters on Earth. When the vehicle arrives on the planet, it first looks at it; Thus, we understand whether the value it measures is really correct and whether the instrument breaks down on the journey. What happens after the data arrives? What landed on Earth from the vehicle is not yet a photograph or a scientific result. Raw data goes through a very long line. Six steps from raw data to published information. It is transmitted first. The data is sent by radio waves and captured by large antennas on Earth. The distance is long, the bandwidth is narrow; Usually the data is recorded, compressed and sent piece by piece in a suitable window. It takes about three to twenty-two minutes for a signal to even arrive from Mars. Then it is cleaned. Each detector has its own flaws: dead pixels, noise from heat, lenses that collect less light at the edges. These known flaws are removed from the data. In addition, if there are broken packages on the way, they are detected and marked. Then it is calibrated. That's where that little calibration target comes in. The raw numbers — "that pixel read 3184" — are translated into meaningful physical units: this much brightness, this much temperature, this much reflection at that wavelength. Then it is positioned. Data alone has no meaning; where and when it belongs. Combined with the vehicle's orbital information, each measurement is linked to a coordinate and a timestamp on the planet's surface. Only after this stage do the images sit on a map. Then it is archived. The resulting data is uploaded to public archives — such as NASA's Planetary Data System and ESA's Planetary Science Archive. This step is more important than it seems: it is returned to that data even decades after the mission is over. New papers are still emerging from Voyager's records in the 1980s today. Commented last. Scientists compare the data in the archive with the models, write their findings, and put them through the peer-review process. A result usually needs to be independently verified to be considered a "discovery". Closing Perhaps my favorite part of this line is this: none of the steps are exciting on their own. Fix broken pixels, flip volumes, tag files. But the fact that we can say that Mars once had a lake or that there was an ocean under Europa's ice is precisely because these tedious steps were done patiently. And that data is there. The archives are open, free to download. The next discoverer doesn't have to send a spacecraft — they just need to open a file that no one is looking at carefully.
