The Sky Didn't Disappear — We Erased It

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · August 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Look up at the night sky from a city center. If you're lucky, you'll count twenty or thirty stars. Drive an hour and a half that same night to a mountain plateau and let your eyes adjust to the dark: now you're looking at thousands of stars, and a Milky Way stretching from one horizon to the other.

What changes between the two isn't the sky. It's the air between us and it.

What is light pollution?

In short: it's the part of artificial light that misses its target. The share of a streetlamp that lights up the sky instead of the road, the glow from a sign leaking through your window, the light a parking lot scatters upward.

That light hits dust and gas particles suspended in the air and scatters in every direction. The result is that faint orange-white dome hanging over cities: sky glow. The stars are still there, but it's like trying to see a candle flame in a brightly lit room — the light exists, you just can't see it.

There's an odd twist here. In recent years, the old yellow lamps have been replaced with white LEDs to save energy. Bills went down — but white light scatters far more in the air, so the sky actually got brighter. Less electricity, more pollution.

Does going somewhere dark actually make a difference?

The difference is bigger than most people expect. Astronomers rate sky darkness on a scale from 1 to 9 — 1 being pristine darkness, 9 being a city center. In practice, it looks like this:

  • In the city (7-9): The Moon, the planets, and a handful of bright stars. No Milky Way to speak of.
  • On the edge of a town or in the suburbs (5-6): The star count rises noticeably. Directly overhead, the Milky Way appears as a faint band of cloud.
  • In the countryside (3-4): The Milky Way is clear. For the first time, constellations really look like a "connect-the-dots" drawing.
  • In true darkness (1-2): The Milky Way is bright enough to cast a shadow on the ground. The Andromeda Galaxy — our neighbor, 2.5 million light-years away — appears to the naked eye as a faint smudge.

Meteor showers are a good example too. A shower's actual rate is the same wherever you are — what changes is how many you're able to count. In the city, seeing five meteors an hour is a good night; in true darkness, fifty.

If you're into photography, the rule is even more unforgiving: heading to a dark-sky location makes far more of a difference than buying expensive gear.

Where do you keep track of all this?

Fortunately, you don't have to guess.

  • To find a dark spot: lightpollutionmap.info is the most useful tool. Find your location on the map and read the surrounding dark spots off the color scale. Dark Site Finder is a similar alternative.
  • For weather: Clear Outside. Unlike regular weather apps, it gives you an hour-by-hour breakdown of what actually matters for observing — cloud layers, humidity, atmospheric steadiness. A dark sky is useless if it's cloudy — always check before you go.
  • To know what you're looking at: Stellarium. Free, available on both phone and desktop. Point your phone at the sky and it tells you what you're looking at; it also lets you plan ahead — "what's rising at what time tonight" — before you even leave home.
  • If you want to contribute: Globe at Night. You look at a designated constellation with the naked eye, count how many stars you can make out, and submit it — your data gets added to a worldwide light-pollution map. No equipment required.

The situation in Turkey

Turkey's main observatory, the TÜBİTAK National Observatory, sits at 2,500 meters on Bakırlıtepe in Saklıkent, Antalya. The location wasn't chosen by accident: high, dry, and surrounded by darkness.

The same logic applies to the rest of us. Getting away from the city isn't enough on its own — you need to head in the right direction. The Mediterranean coastline is bright from end to end; real darkness starts inland, beyond the mountains. Checking the map to find the nearest dark spot to your home takes about ten minutes.

Three things before you go

  • Check the Moon. A full moon can make even the darkest sky look like a city. The best window is the week around the new moon.
  • Be patient. Your eyes take 20-30 minutes to fully adjust to the dark, and a single glance at your phone screen resets that. Switch it to the dimmest red mode, or don't turn it on at all.
  • Dress warm. It's a cliché, but the most common reason observing sessions end early isn't clouds — it's getting cold.

And here's the good news

Light pollution has one thing going for it that other kinds of pollution don't: it isn't permanent.

Carbon stays in the atmosphere for centuries; plastic sits in the ocean waiting to break down. But excess light ends the moment you switch off the lamp. Reversing it doesn't take a decade-long plan.

The solution is already known, and honestly boringly simple: fixtures that point light down instead of up, warmer-colored bulbs, lighting only what's needed and only where it's needed, dimming things late at night. What it takes isn't a technological breakthrough — just intent.

The sky is still there. We just need to pull the curtain back.
