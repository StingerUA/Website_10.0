The Sky's Thermometer: How Do We Tell a Star's

Aslı Bolat · Astronomer and Educator · October 06, 2026 · 6 min read

Temperature from Its Color?

When we look at the night sky, we most often see stars as white points of light. However, if we look a little more carefully, we may notice that some stars shine in bluish tones, some in yellowish tones, and others in orange or red. These color differences are not merely an aesthetic detail. In fact, the colors of stars carry important information about their surface temperatures. So how can we measure the temperature of a star that is millions, even billions, of kilometers away? And without going next to the star and placing a thermometer? The answer lies in light itself.

Blue Is Hot, Red Is Cooler

In everyday life, on temperature indicators red usually means “hot” and blue means “cold.” But when we look at stars, we see exactly the opposite. At the root of this is a physical process called blackbody radiation. When we heat an object, the properties of the light it emits change as its temperature rises. For example, when a piece of metal is heated enough, it first begins to glow red. As the temperature rises further, it can reach orange, yellow, and finally whitish-bluish tones. The same physics applies to stars. In general:

  • Blue stars: Have very high surface temperatures.
  • White and yellow stars: Lie in the medium temperature range.
  • Orange and red stars: Have relatively lower surface temperatures.

For example, the temperature of the Sun's photosphere is around 5,500 °C. By contrast, the surface temperatures of some hot blue stars can exceed 30,000 °C. In red stars, the temperature can drop to a few thousand degrees. Of course, the word “cooler” here does not mean cold in the Earth sense. Even the surface of a red star can be several thousand degrees hot.

So Where Does This Color Difference Come From?

A star emits light at different wavelengths. However, the amount of light it emits is not the same at every wavelength. As temperature increases, the energy distribution of the light the star emits changes, and the wavelength at which the radiation peaks shifts toward shorter wavelengths. One of the fundamental laws of physics that explains this relationship is Wien's Displacement Law. Put simply: As an object's temperature increases, the wavelength at which its radiation peaks becomes shorter.

For this reason, the light of hotter stars shifts toward the blue and violet side of the spectrum, while the light of cooler stars shifts toward the red and infrared side. Astronomers can use this relationship to obtain important information about the temperatures of stars.

Do Astronomers Rely Only on Their Eyes?

Actually, no. It is not possible to determine a star's temperature precisely just by looking at its color with the naked eye. Starlight is collected with telescopes and split into different wavelengths using spectrometers (spectrographs). The resulting spectrum also provides information about the elements in the star's atmosphere. Elements such as hydrogen, helium, calcium, and iron form characteristic absorption or emission lines at specific wavelengths. The distribution and intensity of these lines give clues about the star's temperature and the physical conditions of its atmosphere. Therefore, astronomers do not simply say, “This star looks blue.” They try to reveal its physical properties by analyzing the light coming from the star in detail.

Stellar Temperature Classes: OBAFGKM

The best-known system used in the spectral classification of stars consists of the letters O, B, A, F, G, K, and M. The order runs from the hottest stars to the coolest stars:

Class General color Approximate temperature O Blue More than 30,000 K B Blue-white 10,000–30,000 K A White 7,500–10,000 K F Yellow-white 6,000–7,500 K G Yellow 5,200–6,000 K K Orange 3,700–5,200 K M Red Less than 3,700 K

The interesting thing about this classification is that the letters do not follow alphabetical order. The reason is that the classification system used in modern astronomy emerged historically through the reorganization of different stellar classes. Our Sun is a star of class G2V. Here, “G2” indicates the star's spectral temperature class, while “V” indicates that it is a main-sequence star.

Rigel and Betelgeuse: Two Different Worlds in the Same Constellation

The constellation Orion (the Hunter) is a very good example for seeing this color difference in the sky.

Rigel, one of Orion's bright stars, stands out with its blue-white color. Its high surface temperature places it in a physical world close to the hot stars of classes O and B. Betelgeuse, the other striking star of the constellation, stands out with its distinct orange-red color. Being a red supergiant, Betelgeuse's surface temperature is much lower than Rigel's. Two stars being in the same region of the sky makes a nice example for observing the relationship between star colors and temperature. But there is an important detail here: A star's color does not depend only on its temperature. The star's atmosphere, chemical composition, observing conditions, and the effects of Earth's atmosphere can also change the color we see. For this reason, in professional astronomy, temperature is not determined by looking only at visual color; the star's spectrum and light distribution are examined in detail.

Now You Can Look at the Sky in a Different Way

On your next stargazing session, don't look at the stars in the sky only by their brightness. When you see a blue star, you can think that temperatures of tens of thousands of degrees prevail on its surface, and when you see a reddish star, that it has lower temperatures. In fact, every color we see in the sky carries a small piece of the star's physical world. This is exactly why we can learn a star's temperature from millions of kilometers away: Stars do not merely give off light. Through their light, they send information about themselves. When we learn to read the sky, each of those small points of light turns into a cosmic thermometer that tells of the laws of physics of the universe.
