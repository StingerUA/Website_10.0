Solar Storms: Could the Sun One Day Shut

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · October 11, 2026 · 3 min read

Down Earth's Internet?

The Sun doesn't just give us light and warmth. Every now and then, it also hurls billions of tons of plasma toward Earth. Most of the time, we don't even notice. But sometimes telegraph lines throw sparks, satellites fall out of orbit, or the lights of an entire province go out.

What is a solar storm?

The magnetic field on the Sun's surface occasionally tangles and snaps, releasing a huge amount of energy. This is called a solar flare. Sometimes, along with the flare, a giant cloud of plasma is thrown into space: a coronal mass ejection, or CME for short. When this cloud hits Earth, it shakes our magnetic field, and we call this a geomagnetic storm. A typical CME takes a few days to reach Earth.

The 1859 Carrington Event: The Sky Caught Fire

On September 1, 1859, the astronomer Richard Carrington saw an unusually bright flare on the Sun. The storm that struck about 17 hours later produced spectacular northern lights that reached as far south as the Caribbean. The same storm brought down telegraph systems all around the world, and some operators reported sparks and fires coming from their equipment. At that time, electrical technology was almost nothing but the telegraph. What would happen if it struck today? That is the real question.

What would happen today?

We know part of the answer from history. On March 13, 1989, a much smaller storm brought down the power grid in the Canadian province of Quebec. More than six million people were left without electricity for nine hours, and the economic damage is estimated at about 13.2 billion Canadian dollars.

Satellites are vulnerable too. In February 2022, 38 of the 49 Starlink satellites launched by SpaceX burned up because of geomagnetic storms that were considered mild. In May 2024, the strongest storm in 20 years hit Earth. It was the first G5 (highest level) storm in more than two decades, and nearly half of the satellites in low orbit automatically fired their thrusters to counter the increased atmospheric drag. No major disaster occurred, but it sent scientists a clear message.

The Risk in Numbers

  • Arrival time: The Carrington CME reached Earth in about 17.6 hours. That such a journey, which normally takes days, was this short shows how much the time available to prepare can shrink.
  • Electricity risk: According to a Lloyd's report, a storm of Carrington magnitude could today leave 20-40 million people in the US facing power outages that could last from 16 days to 1-2 years.
  • Economic loss: The same report estimates the total cost for the US at between 0.6 and 2.6 trillion dollars. A 2008 report by the US National Research Council also said it could rise as high as 2 trillion dollars.
  • A narrowly avoided disaster: In July 2012, a Carrington-sized storm narrowly missed Earth. Had that region of the Sun faced Earth just a few days later, the story could have been very different.

So how are we preparing?

The good news: we now watch the Sun continuously. Institutions such as NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center monitor the Sun around the clock with satellites. For example, before the 2022 Starlink incident, the forecast center had already begun issuing its warnings and alerts on January 30. What is missing is the ability to predict the storm's arrival time and intensity more precisely. More measurements and better models are being developed for this.

Power grids have learned their lesson too. After Quebec, the Canadian government invested 1.2 billion dollars to protect the Hydro-Quebec grid. Satellite companies are also accounting for increased atmospheric drag in their new designs.

Closing

The Sun may be a threat, but it is also the owner of the greatest show in the sky. The same storms, as in May 2024, carry the northern lights, normally seen only at the poles, much farther south and call millions of people to look up at the sky. As we get to know the Sun, we also learn to protect ourselves from it and to live with it. The next time the sky turns green, remember that it is both a warning and a gift.
