Aslı Bolat · Astronomer and Educator · September 27, 2026 · 6 min read

Surface?

When you stand near a campfire, physics behaves simply: the farther you move from the flame, the cooler it gets. But when it comes to the Sun, nature doesn't follow the rules we're used to.

The Sun's visible surface, the photosphere, sits at around 5,500°C. Yet as we move away from the Sun into its outer atmosphere — the corona — instead of cooling down, the temperature climbs to millions of degrees. Depending on the region and conditions, the corona's temperature can range from roughly 1 to 3 million °C.

So how can a star's atmosphere get hotter the farther it is from the energy source?

This question, known in astronomy as the Coronal Heating Problem, is one of the most fascinating areas of research in modern astrophysics. We understand how the Sun generates its energy — but we still don't fully understand how that energy is transported into its atmosphere and heats the corona to millions of degrees.

The Sun's Hidden Energy Mechanism

Thinking of the Sun as simply a hot ball of gas isn't enough to understand what happens in its atmosphere. The Sun is a massive sphere of plasma — electrically charged particles in constant motion — and this plasma's movement is deeply intertwined with the star's powerful, ever-changing magnetic fields.

The turbulent motions on the Sun's surface bend, twist, and reshape magnetic field lines. The energy generated in this process can be carried into the Sun's atmosphere through magnetic fields and plasma motion.

Scientists focus on two main mechanisms to explain the corona's extreme heat: Alfvén waves and small-scale energy releases through magnetic reconnection.

  1. Alfvén Waves: Energy Carried by Magnetic Fields

The Sun's visible surface is a constantly moving, boiling layer of plasma. This movement creates vibrations in magnetic field lines. These vibrations can turn into magnetic waves traveling through the plasma, known as Alfvén waves.

These waves can be compared to vibrations traveling along a taut guitar string — except here, it's not a string carrying the vibration, but the physical medium formed jointly by the magnetic field and the plasma. Alfvén waves may play an important role in transporting energy from the Sun's lower atmosphere to its outer layers. Once these waves reach the corona, their energy is transferred to particles through various plasma processes and converted into heat. In particular, wave damping, turbulence, and wave-plasma interactions are among the main processes being studied to explain coronal heating.

However, there's an important caveat: while the existence of Alfvén waves and their capacity to carry energy is supported by observations, exactly how much they heat the corona — and under what conditions and where that energy is released — is still being investigated.

  1. Nanoflares: The Sun's Magnetic Energy Reservoirs

Another major explanation for coronal heating is the release of energy stored in the Sun's magnetic fields through small-scale events.

Motions on the Sun's surface cause magnetic field lines to bend, stretch, and become tangled with one another. From time to time, different magnetic field regions interact and reconnect. In this process, called magnetic reconnection, part of the magnetic energy can be converted into particle motion and heat.

Large-scale examples of this phenomenon are observed in solar flares. But it's thought that much smaller, more frequent energy releases may also play a significant role in keeping the corona continuously hot.

This is where nanoflares come in.

Nanoflares are hypothesized events that could occur in the Sun's atmosphere, releasing far smaller amounts of energy compared to major solar flares. Even though no single one has much impact on its own, if they occur frequently and in large numbers, they could contribute significantly to heating the corona.

Just as a large fire can maintain its heat not only through a few giant flames but through countless small combustion processes, the Sun's corona may also be heated by the combined effect of many small-scale energy releases.

That said, the exact contribution of nanoflares to coronal heating has not yet been determined. Scientists consider that wave-driven heating and magnetic reconnection are not mutually exclusive explanations — they may both be at work together, in different regions and under different conditions.

Parker Solar Probe: A Close Look at the Sun's Secrets

Solving the coronal heating problem requires more than observing the Sun from Earth, since many of the processes occurring in the corona require direct measurement and high-resolution observation.

For this purpose, NASA's Parker Solar Probe — one of the closest observational spacecraft ever sent to the Sun — was designed to study the Sun's outer atmosphere and the solar wind. Launched in 2018, the spacecraft measures magnetic fields, plasma particles, and energy flows during its close passes to the Sun, providing scientists with crucial data on conditions that couldn't previously be studied directly.

Parker Solar Probe's observations are improving our understanding of how the solar wind forms, how magnetic fields behave, and how energy is transferred in the corona. These measurements also play a critical role in testing models that explain how the corona is heated. However, the probe's mission isn't to definitively confirm a single mechanism, but to help us understand more closely the complex physical processes taking place in the Sun's atmosphere.

Why Does the Corona's Temperature Matter for Earth?

The fact that the Sun's outer atmosphere reaches millions of degrees isn't merely a theoretical problem of interest to astronomers. This extreme heat contributes to particles in the Sun's atmosphere reaching very high speeds, with some escaping the Sun's gravity and spreading into space.

This stream of particles is called the solar wind. The solar wind travels throughout the solar system, interacting with the magnetic fields and atmospheres of the planets.

While Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere shield us from much of the solar wind's effects, powerful solar flares and coronal mass ejections can cause serious changes in space weather.

These events can affect satellite operations, GPS and communication systems, the radiation levels astronauts are exposed to, and in some cases, power grids.

Solving the coronal heating problem will help us better understand how energy is transported in the Sun's atmosphere and how the solar wind forms. This knowledge is also an important part of efforts to model space weather and predict potentially dangerous solar events in advance.

Conclusion: Why Is the Sun's Atmosphere Hotter?

The Sun's corona, despite being farther from the star than its surface, reaches temperatures of millions of degrees. This is because the corona isn't heated solely by radiation from the Sun's surface — it's heated by energy transported through magnetic fields and plasma motion, which is then transferred to particles there.

Alfvén waves, magnetic reconnection, and nanoflares are among the key processes being studied to explain this energy transfer. But the relative contributions of these mechanisms, and how they work together in different regions of the corona, are not yet fully resolved.

Perhaps the Sun's greatest mystery lies not in how it produces its energy, but in how it transports that energy into its atmosphere. And as humanity works to understand this invisible energy mechanism of its own star, it continues to better understand not just the Sun, but Earth and its future in space.
