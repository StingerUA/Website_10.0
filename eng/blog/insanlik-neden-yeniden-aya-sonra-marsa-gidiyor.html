Why Humanity Goes to the Moon Again, Then Going to Mars?

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · September 19, 2026 · 4 min read

Going to Mars?

INTRODUCTION

Fifty years ago, Apollo astronauts set foot on the Moon, planted flags, collected a few rock samples, and returned. Then silence. For decades, humanity did not go to the Moon — budgets were cut, interest shifted. So why is it back in the spotlight now? Why are NASA, SpaceX, China and many other players rushing to the Moon at the same time? The answer is simple: this time the goal is not to plant a flag, but to be permanent.

New players, new motivation

In the Apollo era, the race to the Moon was all about Cold War competition — the question of who would go first. Today, the picture is very different. Private companies like SpaceX are on the scene alongside NASA, and that makes a difference. When companies that are not dependent on the state budget, can iterate quickly, and can take risks are involved, costs have decreased and speed has increased. Reusable rockets like Starship have the potential to eliminate the decades-long cost of one-off launches. The Artemis program is the epitome of this new collaboration: NASA's planning combines with the engineering of private companies. The goal is clear — to establish a permanent presence on the Moon, and then to leap to Mars.

A "gas station" on the road

One of the most surprising engineering details is that even a giant rocket like Starship cannot carry the fuel to go to the Moon and back on its own. Because getting to the Moon or Mars is not just going up — it takes a lot of fuel for both the departure, arrival and possible return, and loading that much fuel on the rocket makes it incredibly heavy.

The solution is creative: Starship will first orbit Earth, then receive a fuel transfer from another Starship waiting there — like stopping at a gas station en route. While this idea of "orbital refueling" may seem simple on paper, the safe transfer of liquid fuel from one vehicle to another in space has never been attempted before on this scale. If successful, it will remove one of the biggest obstacles to missions to the Moon and Mars.

What would it be like to live on the moon?

Going to the Moon no longer means just visiting, but staying there. Plans include small habitats on the lunar surface, solar-powered systems, and even building materials from lunar soil. The last one is particularly exciting: instead of transporting everything from Earth, using the resources found on the Moon to produce there — an area that sounds like science fiction, but is being seriously studied. The presence of frozen water in the polar regions of the Moon also makes things easier. Water means the production of drinking water, oxygen and even rocket fuel. In other words, the Moon has the potential to be not just a stop, but a self-sufficient base.

Mars: the real big dream

The Moon is not a goal in this picture, it's a stepping stone. The real target is Mars. Why the Moon first? Because it's a "rehearsal stage" three days away from Earth, with room for error. Habitat technologies, life support systems, long-term mission protocols tried and tested on the Moon — all can be safely tested before heading to Mars. Mars is much more challenging: a journey that takes months, no real rescue possibilities, a much thinner atmosphere. But it also promises a much greater reward — a future where humanity is not dependent on a single planet.

So what will it bring us?

Here's the real cool part. This journey isn't just about conquering space. Technologies developed during the Apollo era have infiltrated our daily lives — lightweight materials, advanced communication systems, even some medical imaging techniques are legacies of that era. Closed-loop life support systems, water recycling, renewable energy solutions developed for Moon and Mars missions

all of which can also shed light on climate and resource problems on Earth. A system that learns to recycle water in space with 100% efficiency could also be a solution to the water crisis in arid regions. Beyond that, the inspiration dimension cannot be ignored. During the Apollo era, a generation of scientists and engineers were inspired by the launches they watched on television in those years. Now the same thing is happening again — millions of young people watching Starship launches on social media are turning to engineering, physics, space sciences. This is the gain of all humanity in the long run. Before the end of the 2030s, we may see a permanent human presence on the Moon and a concrete, tested roadmap to Mars. This is not just going to space — it means that humanity is redefining its own limits, stepping away from being a "one-planet species" and stepping into a more resilient, more curious future. When we look at the Moon, we are no longer just seeing the past, but the future.
