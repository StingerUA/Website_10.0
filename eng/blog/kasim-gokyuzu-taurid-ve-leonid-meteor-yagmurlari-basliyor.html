November Sky: The Taurid and Leonid Meteor

Aslı Bolat · Astronomer and Educator · November 08, 2026 · 4 min read

Showers Begin!

November brings one of the most exciting periods of the year for skywatchers. As two different meteor showers enliven the sky on cool autumn nights, bright fireballs and fast meteor trails will offer observers unique moments. The Taurids will be on skygazers' radar in the first days of November, and the Leonids toward the middle of the month.

So when will these meteor showers peak, where can they be watched from, and what will the viewing conditions be like in 2026?

The Taurids: Slow but Impressive Fireballs

One of the first notable sky events of November is the Taurid meteor shower. These meteors, which appear to come from the direction of the constellation Taurus, are known for their slow movement and for occasionally leaving very bright trails.

The Taurids consist of two separate meteor streams: the Southern Taurids and the Northern Taurids. Both streams are linked to the cosmic dust and particles that the Earth encounters along its orbit.

In 2026, the Southern Taurids reach their maximum activity on the night of November 4–5. The peak of the Northern Taurids is expected on the night of November 11–12.

What makes the Taurids special is not only the number of meteors. Although only a few meteors per hour are expected on average, this stream can occasionally produce very bright fireballs that remain visible in the sky for a long time. It is therefore possible to witness brief but impressive sights in the sky.

The Leonids: Fast Visitors of November Nights

In the second half of November, the Leonid meteor shower takes center stage.

The Leonids, which appear to come from the direction of the constellation Leo, are known for their extremely high speeds. Entering the atmosphere at roughly 71 kilometers per second, these particles can create short but distinct streaks of light in the sky.

The source of the Leonids is comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, which completes its orbit around the Sun roughly every 33 years. Each year, as the Earth passes through the region containing the particles the comet has left behind, the Leonid meteor shower occurs.

In 2026, the Leonids' activity period runs from November 6 to 30. Maximum activity is expected on the night spanning November 17 to 18.

Under favorable viewing conditions, about 15 meteors per hour can be seen. However, this number is a theoretical value calculated for ideal dark-sky conditions; the actual number observed may be lower depending on location and atmospheric conditions.

The Leonids are also known throughout history for extraordinary meteor storms. The displays of 1833 and 1966 in particular are among the unforgettable events in which thousands of meteors were seen in the sky. However, a meteor storm of a similar scale is not expected in 2026.

How Can They Be Observed from Türkiye?

You don't need a telescope or binoculars to observe these meteor showers. In fact, it is more advantageous to see as wide a portion of the sky as possible while watching meteor showers.

For those who want to observe from Türkiye, these recommendations are important:

  • Get away from city lights: If possible, choose rural areas with little light pollution.
  • Make use of the hours after midnight: The hours after midnight and before dawn are more favorable for observation, especially for the Leonids.
  • Give your eyes time: Wait at least 20–30 minutes to adapt to the darkness.
  • Look at the whole sky: Don't limit yourself to the point where the meteors appear to originate. A wide field of view may help you catch more meteors.
  • Be patient: Meteors do not appear at regular intervals. Sometimes you may see nothing for several minutes.

In November 2026, the phase of the Moon also works in observers' favor. In particular, moonlight is expected to be very low during the peak of the Northern Taurids. At the peak of the Leonids, the hours after moonset will offer a darker sky.

Keep Your Eyes on the Sky on November Nights!

The bright fireballs of the Taurids and the high-speed meteors of the Leonids make November special for sky observation. With their different characteristics, both meteor showers remind observers of the trails of cosmic dust that the Earth passes through every year.

Sometimes a small line of light that appears in the sky for only a second is enough to remind us how active and dynamic space is.

This November, as long as the weather permits, don't forget to look up at the sky a little more often. Perhaps the nicest surprise of the night will appear at the very moment you least expect it!
