The Blue Dot on Your Phone Owes a Debt

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · November 08, 2026 · 4 min read

to Einstein: So How Is Position Found in

Space?

When you open the map on your phone, the blue dot shows your exact location within seconds. This little dot relies on the clocks of satellites in space and on Einstein's theory of relativity. So what happens once you leave Earth? Is there GPS in space?

How does GPS work?

GPS satellites constantly broadcast a signal saying “the time is now this.” Your phone looks at how much delay the signals from several satellites arrive with, and multiplies this delay by the speed of light to find your distance to each satellite. When the distances are intersected, your position comes out. The precision is incredible: an error of one microsecond (one millionth of a second) in the signal means a distance error of about 300 meters.

Einstein steps in

GPS satellites orbit at an altitude of about 20,184 km above Earth, and two relativistic effects throw off their clocks. Because Earth's gravity is weaker up there, satellite clocks run about 45 microseconds per day faster according to general relativity. The satellites' high speed, on the other hand, slows the clocks by about 7 microseconds per day according to special relativity. The result: satellite clocks run ahead of those on the ground by about 38 microseconds per day.

38 microseconds, multiplied by the speed of light, comes to about 11 kilometers. If it were not corrected, an error of about 11 km per day would accumulate in distance measurements. Engineers solve this in advance: the satellite clock's frequency is lowered from 10.23 MHz to 10.22999999543 MHz before launch, so that it produces the correct frequency once in space. In other words, your phone showing the right place depends on Einstein being right.

So is there GPS in space?

Sort of. GPS works in orbits close to Earth; even the NICER instrument on the ISS has its own GPS position. The problem starts as you move farther away. GPS satellites beam most of their power toward Earth's surface. Only a weak signal leaking from the sides reaches faraway places like the Moon.

GPS on the Moon: The First Attempt Worked

NASA and the Italian Space Agency's LuGRE experiment tested this problem. The experiment was launched on January 15, 2025 aboard Firefly's Blue Ghost lander and landed on the Moon on March 2, 2025. The receiver picked up GPS and Galileo signals both in lunar orbit and on the surface: signal tracking was achieved in orbit about 395,900 km from Earth, and an autonomous position solution on the surface about 362,100 km away. The previous record, in 2019, was about 192,500 km. So in the future, astronauts and rovers on the Moon could benefit from the same satellite navigation.

In deep space, Earth mostly finds the position

Farther out, GPS doesn't work. The position of most probes is determined by giant antennas on Earth (the Deep Space Network):

  • Distance: The time it takes a signal to travel to the probe and back is measured.
  • Speed: The shift in the signal frequency (Doppler) shows the probe's speed.

There is one exception: the Deep Space 1 probe became the first spacecraft to photograph asteroids and stars and calculate its own position from the asteroid's apparent position relative to the stars.

The future: Navigation with pulsars

Another method is pulsars: rapidly spinning neutron stars that send out beams of light at very regular intervals. NASA's SEXTANT experiment tracked four millisecond pulsars with the NICER telescope aboard the ISS and made 78 timing measurements over two days. The goal was to find the position within a 16 km radius. Two weeks had been allowed, and the team achieved this in about 8 hours. In most of the later data, the error dropped below 4.8 km.

Positioning in space by the numbers

  • GPS orbital altitude: about 20,184 km
  • Satellite clock difference: about 38 microseconds per day (45 − 7)
  • Distance error if uncorrected: about 11 km per day
  • LuGRE orbit tracking / surface position: about 395,900 km / 362,100 km
  • Pulsar navigation (SEXTANT): 78 measurements, 2 days, error below 4.8 km in most data

Closing

On Earth, the blue dot; on the Moon, a weak GPS signal; in deep space, radio antennas; perhaps pulsars tomorrow. As space expands, so do the ways of knowing where we are. Next time you open the map on your phone, remember this: that little dot is Einstein's equations in your pocket.
