We're Turning Space into a Junkyard: Are Future Spaceflights at Risk?

Alba Space Team · Space Researchers · 9 August 2026 · 4 min read

When we look at the sky, we imagine space as infinite and completely empty. But the orbits around Earth are not as empty as we think. As a result of human space missions carried out over decades, thousands of satellites, rocket fragments and debris of various sizes are moving around Earth. Some of these objects are no longer in use. Yet they continue to exist in orbit.

What Is Space Debris?

Space debris is the general term for human-made objects in Earth orbit that no longer serve any function. Examples include satellites that have reached the end of their operational lives, upper stages of rockets, mission-related fragments and small particles generated by collisions. The fundamental danger of these pieces comes from their speed. A piece of debris in orbit can travel at speeds of kilometers per second. For that reason, even an object only a few centimetres across can cause severe damage if it strikes an operational satellite or spacecraft.

The Real Danger: Kessler Syndrome

One of the most striking concepts when discussing space pollution is the Kessler Syndrome. Proposed in 1978 by NASA scientist Donald J. Kessler and Burton G. Cour-Palais, this scenario holds that if the density of objects in Earth orbit reaches a certain level, collisions can cascade. Imagine two large pieces of debris colliding: the impact can produce hundreds or thousands of new fragments. Some of those fragments may strike other satellites, creating yet more debris. Thus a single collision can become a chain reaction that triggers further collisions over time. In the worst-case Kessler Syndrome scenario, the density of debris in certain orbits could rise so high that those regions become extremely hazardous for new space missions.

How Does This Problem Affect Earth?

Space debris is not a problem only for astronauts. Many technologies in our daily lives depend on satellites. We rely on satellites for GPS and navigation systems, communications, weather forecasting, television broadcasts and Earth observation. Losing the safety of orbital space could therefore affect the future of systems that depend on space technologies. Moreover, to safely carry out planned missions to the Moon, Mars and beyond, the orbits around Earth must be kept under control.

Is It Possible to Clean Up Space?

The solution to this problem is not only to stop launching new satellites. A large fraction of existing space debris is already in orbit. Scientists and space agencies are developing various technologies to track these objects, predict risky conjunctions in advance and safely remove some large pieces from orbit. In the future, robotic arms, net systems or other capture technologies aim to clear certain large debris items. At the same time, safer designs are being developed so that new satellites do not remain in orbit once their missions end.

The Space of the Future Begins Today

Humanity sends more satellites and spacecraft into space every day. This creates major opportunities across communications and science. But increased use of space brings a new responsibility. We must think of space not only as a place to explore but as an environment to protect. If we want to travel to Mars, build bases on the Moon, or explore other planets in the future, we must first keep the routes around Earth safe. The fact that space is vast does not mean our debris are inconsequential.
