Sleeping in Space: The 16 Dawns of a Day on the ISS

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · September 27, 2026 · 3 min read

On Earth, the sun rises once and sets once a day. On the International Space Station (ISS), however, this happens 16 times — because the station orbits the Earth once every 90 minutes. So for an astronaut, the concept of a "day" as we know it completely disappears. So how does the human body understand when it's "night" in an environment where the sun rises and sets this often?

The moment the body gets confused

The human body has an internal clock that is the product of millions of years of evolution — the circadian rhythm. This rhythm works through cells in the retina sensing daylight: as light decreases, the brain begins secreting melatonin, and we prepare for sleep. But when day and night each last only 45 minutes on the ISS, this system gets completely confused. The brain can't find a clear answer to the question "should I sleep now, or stay awake now?" The result: many astronauts experience serious sleep problems in the first weeks after going to space. Some studies show that astronauts sleep on average 2 hours less in orbit than they do on Earth.

NASA's solution: creating an artificial day

There's a simple but effective solution for managing this chaos on the ISS: astronauts stick to a 24-hour schedule just like on Earth — generally based on Greenwich Mean Time (GMT). The windows are blacked out, and the cabin's LED lighting system emits light at different color temperatures throughout the day: bluer/cooler tones in the morning (to trigger wakefulness), warmer/orange tones in the evening (to support melatonin release). This artificially signals to the body "it's daytime now" or "it's nighttime now." During sleep, since there's no gravity in space, astronauts get into sleeping bags fixed to the wall so they don't float around — inside personal cabins about the size of a phone booth. Some wear headphones, some use eye masks, and some even resort to sleeping pills.

Real astronaut experiences

Scott Kelly, during his year-long mission on the ISS, repeatedly stated that his sleep schedule was one of the most difficult parts — especially in the first weeks of the mission. Chris Hadfield, in his autobiography, describes how the concept of "night" in space becomes a kind of artificial agreement — turning off the lights at 22:00 and saying "it's time for us to sleep now" is, in a way, a form of collective self-deception. While some astronauts adapt to this irregularity over time, others experience chronic fatigue throughout the mission. This is exactly one of the topics most studied by NASA's Human Health and Performance division: the effect of sleep quality on mission performance, attention span, and decision-making speed.

Why does this matter so much?

ISS missions last anywhere from a few months to a year. But eyes are now turned toward Mars — and a round trip to Mars could take two to three years. Living with a disrupted sleep pattern for such a long period isn't just uncomfortable, it's a serious safety risk. A tired mind can make a wrong decision at a critical moment. That's why NASA and partner agencies are working on more advanced artificial lighting systems, personalized sleep protocols, and even research into medications/supplements that regulate biological rhythms for future deep-space missions.

Closing

Every "night" on the ISS is actually a small battle waged by human ingenuity against nature. Learning to sleep in a place where the sun rises and sets 16 times may be one of the most human lessons that humanity — on its way to Mars — still needs to learn.
