Making Tools from a Printer in Space: The Era of 3D Printing on the ISS

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · October 03, 2026 · 3 min read

Age of 3D Printing on the ISS

Imagine this: you are working 400 kilometers above Earth with only one wrench, and it breaks. You can't buy a new one at a store, and there is no cargo delivery either. It could be months before a new wrench arrives. That is exactly why astronauts now make tools not in their pockets, but in a printer inside the station.

First step: The first printer sent to space

Made In Space's Zero-G printer was launched to the ISS aboard SpaceX CRS-4 on September 21, 2014, and the first part manufactured in space was printed on November 24, 2014. Ironically, this first part was the printer's own spare part: an extruder cover. In other words, from day one the machine was saying, “I'll repair myself.”

The real excitement came a few weeks later. The tool was designed on Earth, emailed to the station, and the ratchet wrench that Commander Butch Wilmore needed was printed in four hours. Approving the design and sending it to space took less than a week, and this wrench was one of the 20 objects the printer produced in its first phase. The phrase “emailing a tool to space” sounds not like science fiction, but like a real workflow.

So what is being printed now?

Printers have advanced since then. The ISS now has devices that work with advanced polymers. These devices have produced tools, medical devices, radiation shields, and experimental parts.

One important finding is this: microgravity does not meaningfully affect this printing method from an engineering standpoint. In other words, the printer works in space just as it does on Earth. This shows that the system is reliable.

Lunar base: The next stop

This idea is not limited to the ISS. Instead of transporting everything to the Moon from Earth, the goal is to print construction materials from the dust and rock (regolith) on the lunar surface. ICON's NASA-backed Project Olympus aims to develop autonomous construction systems using lunar regolith. The idea is simple: use the materials you find on the Moon to print landing pads, walls, and shelters. If this succeeds, the cargo that must be carried from Earth to build a base could be reduced significantly.

Why does this matter so much? Let's look at the numbers

  • Spare parts transported: More than 7,000 pounds (about 3,200 kg) of spare parts are sent to the ISS every year. In addition, about 13,000 kg of spare hardware is on the station, and about 17,700 kg is waiting on Earth.
  • Launch cost: Carrying one kilogram of cargo to low Earth orbit has traditionally cost more than $10,000. By a rough calculation, the annual spare-parts shipment alone comes to about $32 million per year.
  • Waiting time: Months with the traditional method, hours with the printer. In the wrench example, it took less than a week from design to printing, and the printing itself took 4 hours.
  • The Mars reality: The Moon can be reached in three days, but a trip to Mars takes months. If something breaks along the way, sending “emergency cargo” is not possible. That is why being able to manufacture the needed part on site is not a luxury, but part of the mission plan.

So the 3D printer is not just a cool gadget. It changes the cost, the safety, and the distance that missions can reach.

Closing

Today we print a ratchet wrench on the ISS. Tomorrow, a shelter on the Moon, and perhaps one day an entire colony on Mars. One day, astronauts will produce what they need on the spot, and space will not be a place far from our home, but an extension of it. The moment the printer's “start” button is pressed is also the moment that future begins.
