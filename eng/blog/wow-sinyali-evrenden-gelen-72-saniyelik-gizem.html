The Wow! Signal: A 72-Second Mystery

Ali Batuhan Özsürmeli · Guest Writer · October 22, 2026 · 3 min read

from the Universe

On the night of August 15, 1977, a radio telescope in Ohio was listening to the sky as usual. The telescope was only recording; no one was waiting at a screen. A few days later, astronomer Jerry Ehman stopped at one line while going through the computer printouts. A sequence of letters and numbers was far stronger than it should have been. He circled it in red pen and wrote a single word in the margin: “Wow!”

What happened that night?

Ohio State University’s “Big Ear” radio telescope was scanning the sky for a SETI (search for extraterrestrial intelligence) program that ran from 1973 to 1997. As the Earth rotated, each point passing through the telescope’s field of view stayed there for about 72 seconds. During that time, the telescope picked up a very strong signal, concentrated at a single frequency, coming from the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. The signal’s intensity first rose to a peak and then slowly faded. A signal from a source fixed in the sky is expected to change in exactly this way as the Earth rotates. This was therefore a strong indication that the signal was not interference from Earth.

Why did people ask “Is it aliens?”

Two things caught everyone’s attention. First, the signal’s frequency was about 1420 MHz. This is the natural frequency emitted by hydrogen, the most abundant element in the universe. If an extraterrestrial civilization were to choose a “universal meeting point,” it could choose this frequency, which every scientist would listen to. Second, the signal was within a very narrow frequency range. Most natural radio sources spread across a wide band, whereas a signal squeezed into a single point this tightly looks more “artificial.”

The “6EQUJ5” sequence that Ehman circled also conveys this intensity. Each character shows how strong the signal was relative to the noise during one 12-second measurement by the telescope. The “U” in this sequence is one of the highest levels on the scale and corresponds to a signal about 30 times stronger than the noise.

Why was it never heard again?

This is where the big problem begins. Big Ear had two separate receivers, and they saw the same point in the sky about 3 minutes apart. The signal appeared in only one of these receivers. In other words, it lasted 72 seconds and then vanished. According to researchers, the signal must have lasted 24 hours at most. The telescope looked at the same spot again and again for months but never found anything. Other telescopes also listened to the same direction for years, and the results again came up empty.

Wow! by the Numbers

  • Date: August 15, 1977
  • Duration: about 72 seconds
  • Frequency: about 1420 MHz (near the hydrogen line)
  • Signal strength: about 30 times the noise
  • Repeats: 0. The signal was never detected again.
  • End of the telescope: Big Ear was demolished in early 1998 by land developers.

So what could it be?

Many explanations have been proposed over the years. In 2017, an amateur researcher suggested that the signal came from the hydrogen cloud of a comet passing in front of the telescope. This idea remained controversial and was not found convincing enough by many astronomers. A more recent study re-examined Big Ear’s archival data. According to this study, published in 2025, the signal’s position and characteristics were determined more precisely, and it was shown that small, cold hydrogen clouds can produce similar narrow-band signals. The researchers say these results support a natural cosmic source rather than artificial interference. So perhaps Wow! is not a message from aliens, but evidence that the universe holds more, and stranger, natural phenomena than we know.

Conclusion

The story of the Wow! signal actually tells us something good: every time we look at the universe, we can still be surprised. Whether the answer is an alien civilization or a natural phenomenon we have never known, those 72 seconds have been pushing scientists for half a century to build better telescopes and to listen more carefully. Perhaps the next “Wow!” is already on its way. This time, someone is keeping a record of it.
