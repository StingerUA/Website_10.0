Did Life Begin on Mars? – Dr. Robert Zubrin's Book Review

İdris Albayrak · Space Entrepreneur, Futurist, and Educator · 1 August 2026 · 5 min read

Could life have begun on Mars? In this thought-provoking review, Dr. Robert Zubrin examines Dr. Steven Benner's new book and the way it addresses one of planetary science's biggest questions. There is also an opportunity to meet Dr. Benner in person at the 2026 International Mars Society Convention; he will deliver a keynote address there, sign copies of his books, and participate in a special panel discussion on this fascinating topic.

By Dr. Robert Zubrin, National Review, 07.11.26

Steve Benner is one of the leading figures in both theoretical and applied biology. A founder of the field of synthetic biology, Benner was the first person to synthesize a gene and is also among the pioneers of the RNA world hypothesis for the origin of life. He is a successful biotechnology entrepreneur; this matters because his wealth has given him the freedom to speak openly even when his views conflict with those of NASA and other members of the scientific community.

His splendid new book "Meet the Neighbors: Life on Mars and How to Find It" is a case in point: drawing on both his own research and an encyclopedic knowledge of the astrobiology community's work, Benner makes no concessions in reaching bold conclusions. Amassing impressive evidence, Benner argues boldly that life almost certainly exists on Mars and that life arose on the Red Planet before it appeared on Earth.

NASA's public relations department has always claimed in statements supporting various Mars missions that their aim was to search for life. They said this because they knew the American public — rightly — cared most about the prospect of extraterrestrial life when it came to Mars. Unfortunately, NASA failed to keep that promise.

In fact, America's space agency has not sent any instrument to detect life on Mars since the Viking mission that landed on the Red Planet on July 20, 1976, roughly half a century ago. Since then, NASA has sent orbiters, stationary landers, and rovers to Mars. While these missions have provided very useful data about the Martian environment, none of them attempted to search for life itself.

However, the Viking landers did carry a fourth instrument known as a Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometer (GCMS). The GCMS assessed the soil's chemical composition and reported no detection of organic material in Martian soil. This was quite surprising for NASA scientists because, even if Mars were lifeless, they expected to find some organics—at least those delivered by meteorites, which typically contain robust organic compounds known as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) that can survive impact.
